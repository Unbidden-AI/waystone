# legal / employment_dispute

Diana: Welcome, Priya. Thanks for coming in. Please, tell me what brings you here today.

Priya: Thank you, Diana. I was terminated from TechSolutions Inc. last week, and I believe it was direct retaliation for an internal HR complaint I filed about gender pay disparity.

Diana: I see. That's a serious allegation. Let's start with a timeline. When did you file the HR complaint, and when were you terminated?

Priya: I submitted the formal complaint to Mr. Henderson in HR on March 15th of this year. My termination meeting was on May 10th.

Diana: Okay, so roughly two months between the complaint and termination. What exactly did your complaint detail?

Priya: I provided data showing that male colleagues with similar experience and roles in the software development department were earning 15-20% more than me. I asked for an explanation and an adjustment.

Diana: And what was TechSolutions' response to your complaint? Did they conduct an investigation?

Priya: Mr. Henderson acknowledged receipt, and I had one follow-up meeting with him on April 2nd. He said they were "looking into it," but I never heard anything concrete after that.

Diana: Were there any changes in your work environment or performance reviews between March 15th and May 10th? Any disciplinary actions or new criticisms?

Priya: That's the thing. My last performance review, in January 2023, was excellent – I received the highest rating. After the complaint, my manager, Mark Johnson, became noticeably colder. He started nitpicking my work, which he'd never done before.

Diana: Do you have copies of that excellent performance review, any emails related to your HR complaint, or any communications from Mark Johnson regarding his criticisms?

Priya: Yes, I have the January performance review. I also have the email confirming my complaint submission and a few emails from Mark with vague criticisms, though nothing formal. I can try to retrieve more.

Diana: Excellent. Those documents will be crucial. Based on what you've told me, your primary claim here would likely be retaliation, rather than just wrongful termination or pure pay discrimination.

Priya: What's the difference? I thought it was all "wrongful."

Diana: "Wrongful termination" is a broad term. Here, the key is that you engaged in a "protected activity" – complaining about pay disparity, which is protected under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act and our state's Fair Employment Practices Act. If your termination was a direct result of that protected activity, that's retaliation. It's often easier to prove than pure discrimination, especially when the timing is so close.

Priya: So, what are the next steps? Do we sue them immediately?

Diana: Not immediately. For federal claims like Title VII, we first need to file a charge of discrimination with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, or EEOC. For state claims, we'd file with our state's equivalent agency.

Priya: How long does that take? And what's the deadline?

Diana: You generally have 180 days from the date of the discriminatory act – in your case, your termination on May 10th – to file with the EEOC, though in some states with their own agencies, it can be extended to 300 days. We'll want to file well within that window. The EEOC will investigate, and then issue a "Right to Sue" letter, which allows us to proceed with a lawsuit.

Priya: What kind of damages could I be looking at if we win?

Diana: Potential damages include back pay – the wages you've lost since termination – and potentially front pay if you can't find a comparable job quickly. We could also seek damages for emotional distress, and in some cases, punitive damages to punish the employer. Attorney's fees are also often recoverable.

Priya: That sounds like a long, expensive process. Is there a way to resolve this without going to court?

Diana: Absolutely. Many cases settle before or during litigation. Once we've filed with the EEOC and they've investigated, or even after we file a lawsuit, settlement discussions often begin. It's a balance between the strength of your case, the costs of litigation, and your desire for a quicker, confidential resolution.

Priya: What are the pros and cons of settlement versus going to trial?

Diana: Settlement offers certainty, confidentiality, and avoids the stress and time commitment of a trial. However, you might not get the full amount you'd potentially win at trial. Litigation offers the chance for a larger award, but it's public, lengthy, expensive, and there's always the risk of losing. We'll discuss this in detail as the case progresses.

Priya: Okay, that makes sense. So, what do I need to do now?

Diana: First, I'd like you to gather all those documents we discussed: the performance review, any emails related to your complaint, and any communications from Mark Johnson. Also, please write down a detailed narrative of everything that happened, with as many dates and names as you can recall.

Priya: I can do that.

Diana: Great. Once I review those, we can finalize our strategy, and if you decide to move forward, we'll sign a retainer agreement. We'll then prepare your EEOC charge.
